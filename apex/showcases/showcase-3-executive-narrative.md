## 1. CLIENT CONTEXT

Schuler Industrietech AG is a precision industrial technology company based in Winterthur, founded in 1978 by Hans-Peter Schuler, who built the firm into a respected supplier of custom automation components to Swiss and German industrial clients. The company is now led by his son, Markus Schuler, who joined the business in 2007 after completing an engineering degree at ETH Zürich and an MBA at St. Gallen. Under Markus's leadership, Schuler Industrietech has expanded from a primarily domestic Swiss business into a DACH-wide supplier, added a CNC precision machining division, and grown revenue from CHF 18M to CHF 34M over eight years.

This is not a startup. This is a 46-year-old company with a spotless credit history, consistent profitability, and a client base that includes four DAX-listed industrial groups. Schuler Industrietech does not need to prove it can operate — it has been operating, successfully, for nearly half a century. What it needed was a financing document that could communicate, in the language that Swiss cantonal banks and ZKB relationship managers understand, that its planned expansion is a sound investment of borrowed capital, that the risk is manageable, and that the people running the business are exactly the right stewards of a CHF 8M facility.

APEX was engaged specifically because Markus Schuler understood that his company's existing communication materials — a corporate brochure, an annual report, and a financial summary prepared by the company's fiduciary — were not built for this purpose. A financing conversation at this scale requires a strategic document, not a collection of operational materials stapled together.

---

## 2. THE CHALLENGE

Schuler Industrietech had three things that most financing applicants do not: strong financials, a long track record, and a clearly defined expansion plan. What it did not have was a narrative that connected those three elements into a coherent strategic argument. The existing materials communicated facts without conveying meaning. A bank reading the fiduciary's financial summary would know that Schuler Industrietech was profitable. It would not know why the expansion was strategically timely, why the company was positioned to capture market opportunity, or why CHF 8M was the right amount at this specific moment.

**Problem 1: The expansion case existed only in the CEO's head.** Markus Schuler had a clear, well-reasoned view of why expanding manufacturing capacity by 40% in 2026 was the right strategic move: an existing German tier-one client had offered a preferred supplier agreement conditional on Schuler's ability to increase output, EU industrial reshoring trends were driving demand for Swiss-precision DACH suppliers, and two competitors had exited the precision machining segment in the last 18 months, leaving a gap in the market. This was a compelling strategic argument. None of it was written down in a form that a bank credit committee could evaluate.

**Problem 2: The financials needed narrative context.** Three years of strong financials (CHF 34M revenue, 14% EBIT margin, zero net debt) are a strong foundation. But a credit committee evaluating a CHF 8M facility needs to understand the financial story: what drove the growth, what the expansion changes in the cost structure, and what the financial covenants look like under conservative assumptions. Numbers without narrative leave the bank's analyst to draw their own conclusions — and analysts are trained to draw conservative ones.

**Problem 3: The document needed to speak the right language.** Swiss cantonal banks and ZKB's corporate lending division operate within a specific risk culture. They are not VC investors evaluating upside potential. They are credit professionals evaluating downside risk. A financing document written in the language of growth and opportunity will fail in this context. It must be written in the language of stability, coverage ratios, and risk mitigation. The same facts, framed differently, land in entirely different positions on a credit committee's approval matrix.

## 3. APEX APPROACH

APEX's approach to the Schuler Industrietech engagement was fundamentally different from a startup pitch engagement. There was no storytelling arc to design, no investor psychology to engineer, and no fundraising urgency to communicate. The task was to build a document that a senior ZKB relationship manager could present to her credit committee with confidence — a document where every claim was supported, every projection was conservative, and every risk was named and addressed before the bank's analyst had to find it themselves.

**Document philosophy: Trust through transparency, not through optimism.** APEX's instruction to the Schuler team was to include everything the bank would want to know, including things that were unflattering. A credit document that acknowledges risks and explains mitigation is more credible than one that presents an uninterrupted picture of strength. Swiss bank credit professionals are trained to find problems. A document that has already found the problems and addressed them makes their job easier — and makes approval more likely.

**Audience calibration: Write for the credit committee, not the relationship manager.** The ZKB relationship manager was already convinced — she was the one who recommended that Schuler Industrietech formalize a financing document. The document's real audience was the three-person credit committee that would review it independently. These are people who have read hundreds of financing applications. The document needed to be structured so that a committee member who spent 15 minutes with it could form a positive view, and a committee member who spent 60 minutes with it could find no gap in the argument.

**Format decision: A written document, not slides.** APEX made the explicit recommendation against a PowerPoint presentation for this engagement. Bank financing conversations at the CHF 8M level in Switzerland are conducted through written documents — formal, structured, paginated, with numbered sections and an appendix. A presentation deck would have signaled unfamiliarity with the conventions of Swiss corporate banking. The document format itself was a credibility signal.

## 4. DOCUMENT STRUCTURE

*Format: 12 pages · Professional A4 document · Formal Swiss business German with English summary available*

---

**Section 1 — Executive Summary (1 page)**
A one-page summary covering: company identity and history in two sentences, the financing request (CHF 8M, purpose, structure), the strategic rationale in three bullet points, financial health indicators (revenue, EBIT, debt position), and the proposed repayment structure. Written to be self-contained — a credit committee member should be able to read this page alone and understand the entire request. The executive summary is not a soft opener; it is a precise statement of the financing case. The first sentence: "Schuler Industrietech AG requests a CHF 8M term facility to expand manufacturing capacity at its Winterthur facility, enabling the fulfilment of a confirmed preferred supplier agreement with [Client Name] and positioning the company to serve accelerating DACH demand for Swiss-precision automation components."

**Section 2 — Company Overview and History**
A factual, confident review of Schuler Industrietech's 46-year operating history: founding, first-generation leadership, transition to second generation in 2012, key milestones (CNC division launch, German market entry, DAX client wins), and current operational profile. This section establishes one thing: this company has survived and grown through multiple economic cycles, two recessions, and a global pandemic without a credit event. That track record is the foundation of the entire financing argument. One page maximum.

**Section 3 — Strategic Position**
An honest assessment of Schuler Industrietech's competitive position in the DACH precision manufacturing market. Three elements: what the company does better than competitors (Swiss precision standards, flexible customization for complex components, 46-year client relationships), what market conditions favor the company right now (EU industrial reshoring, competitor exits, preferred supplier demand from DAX clients), and what the company's strategic intent is for the next five years (become the preferred Swiss-precision automation component supplier for the DACH automotive and industrial robotics supply chain). This section answers the bank's implicit question: is this a company with a future, or one managing a slow decline?

**Section 4 — The Expansion Case**
The strategic and commercial argument for the specific expansion being financed. Three components: (1) the confirmed preferred supplier agreement from the German tier-one client, including the volume commitment and the capacity requirement that triggered the expansion decision, (2) the facility expansion plan — 2,400 sqm new production hall on Schuler's owned Winterthur site, two additional CNC machining centers, and one automated quality control station — with total capital cost breakdown, and (3) the timeline from financing approval to first production at expanded capacity (14 months). This section makes the case that the expansion is not speculative: it is demand-led, capacity-constrained, and already partially committed to by a named client.

**Section 5 — Market Analysis**
A two-page market analysis covering the DACH precision manufacturing segment. Includes: market size and growth rate (sourced from Swissmechanic industry reports and VDMA data), specific demand drivers (automotive electrification requiring new automation component specifications, industrial robotics growth in German manufacturing), and the reshoring trend — EU and Swiss industrial policy increasingly favors domestic supply chain capacity over Asian sourcing for precision components. This section is written conservatively: market growth rates are cited at the lower end of analyst ranges, and no claim is made that is not supported by a named source.

**Section 6 — Competitive Landscape**
A clear-eyed assessment of Schuler Industrietech's competitive environment. Names two primary competitors (anonymized by size and geography), explains the recent exits from the precision machining segment, and addresses the competitive threat from lower-cost Eastern European manufacturers directly — including Schuler's strategy for remaining premium-differentiated (Swiss quality certification, proximity to German clients, 46-year relationship network). Banks appreciate honesty about competition. A document that pretends competition does not exist is less credible than one that acknowledges it and explains why it is manageable.

**Section 7 — Financial Performance (3 Years)**
A three-year financial summary covering FY2023, FY2024, and FY2025. For each year: Revenue, Gross Profit, EBIT, EBIT margin, Net Profit, Total Assets, Equity, Net Debt/Equity ratio, and Operating Cash Flow. Presented in a clean, professionally formatted table. A brief management commentary paragraph accompanies the table explaining the key drivers of each year's performance, any one-time items, and the trend narrative. FY2025 numbers: CHF 34M revenue, 14% EBIT margin, CHF 4.76M EBIT, zero net debt, CHF 3.2M operating cash flow. All figures are drawn from audited financial statements prepared by the company's external auditors.

**Section 8 — Loan Purpose and Use of Funds**
A precise, itemized use-of-funds table. Line items: construction costs for the new production hall (CHF 3.2M), two CNC machining centers (CHF 2.8M), automated quality control station (CHF 1.1M), electrical and infrastructure fit-out (CHF 600K), project management and installation (CHF 300K). Total: CHF 8.0M. Each line item includes a supplier quote reference or cost-per-square-meter benchmark. The bank's analyst should be able to verify every number. No contingency funds or vaguely described "working capital" items. Precision in the use-of-funds table signals that the company has done the work — and that the CHF 8M number is not a rounded estimate but a calculated requirement.

**Section 9 — Financial Projections (3 Years)**
Three-year forward projections for FY2026, FY2027, and FY2028, presented in two scenarios: base case and conservative case (no optimistic scenario — banks do not need to see upside, they need to believe the conservative case is realistic). Base case assumptions: the preferred supplier agreement delivers committed volumes from Q2 2027, overall revenue grows 18% in year one post-expansion and 12% in subsequent years, EBIT margin maintains at 13–14%. Conservative case assumptions: supplier agreement volumes are 25% lower than committed, revenue grows only 8% in the first year, and EBIT margin compresses to 11% due to higher-than-expected operating costs in the new facility. In both scenarios, debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) exceeds 1.8x throughout the loan term. The key table is the annual debt service waterfall showing that operating cash flow comfortably services the proposed CHF 8M facility in every year under both scenarios.

**Section 10 — Risk Analysis and Mitigation**
A formal risk register with five named risks, each assessed for likelihood, impact, and the specific mitigation measure in place. Risk 1: Key client volume shortfall (likelihood: low, given contractual commitment; mitigation: minimum volume clause in preferred supplier agreement). Risk 2: Construction delay (likelihood: medium; mitigation: fixed-price construction contract with penalty clauses, 2-month buffer built into timeline). Risk 3: CNC equipment delivery delay (likelihood: low; mitigation: equipment ordered with 18-month lead time from Swiss supplier, deposit paid). Risk 4: Interest rate increase on variable portion of facility (likelihood: medium; mitigation: 60% of facility proposed at fixed rate). Risk 5: Key personnel dependency — Markus Schuler (likelihood: acknowledged; mitigation: succession plan documented, COO promoted in 2024 with full operational authority). This section demonstrates that management has thought rigorously about what could go wrong and has taken specific steps to address each scenario.

**Section 11 — Management Team**
Brief professional profiles of the three members of the executive management team: Markus Schuler (CEO), the COO who joined in 2018 with a background in precision manufacturing operations, and the CFO who has been with the company since 2015 and has managed all banking relationships and financial reporting. Each profile is two to three sentences: role, relevant background, and tenure. The board of directors is noted: includes two external members, one of whom is a former Sulzer AG operations executive. This section is not about impressive credentials — it is about demonstrating that the management structure is professional, that key functions are not solely dependent on the founder, and that the board provides external oversight.

**Section 12 — Appendix**
Referenced throughout the document but not paginated as part of the main narrative. Contains: three years of audited financial statements in full, the expansion project plan with timeline and supplier quotes, the preferred supplier agreement term sheet (redacted where commercially sensitive), the facility plan and site drawings for the new production hall, Swissmechanic and VDMA market data sources, and insurance certificates for existing and planned assets. The appendix is organized to mirror the main document — each section of the appendix corresponds to a section of the narrative, so the bank's analyst can move between narrative claims and supporting evidence without searching.

## 5. NARRATIVE ARCHITECTURE NOTE

The financing document for Schuler Industrietech was built around what APEX calls the Credibility–Case–Coverage architecture — a structure designed specifically for conservative financial audiences whose primary evaluation framework is risk, not opportunity.

**Credibility (Sections 1–3):** The document opens by establishing, as quickly and economically as possible, that Schuler Industrietech is exactly what it appears to be: a long-established, consistently profitable, debt-free Swiss industrial company with a management team that has demonstrated operational competence across multiple market cycles. For a bank credit committee, the first question is "is this borrower credible?" Every element of the first three sections is designed to answer that question affirmatively before the financing case is presented.

**Case (Sections 4–6):** The middle of the document makes the specific argument for the expansion. Critically, it leads with demand rather than aspiration. The document does not say "we believe there is a market opportunity" — it says "a specific client has made a specific commitment conditional on our capacity expansion." Demand-led expansion is categorically less risky than aspiration-led expansion, and every word of the case section was written to make that distinction unmistakable.

**Coverage (Sections 7–11):** The final section of the main document demonstrates, with financial evidence and under conservative assumptions, that the proposed facility can be serviced comfortably from operating cash flow. The DSCR analysis in the projections is the single most important number in the document for a bank credit committee. Everything else in the document builds the case that the projections are credible. The risk register provides the final layer of comfort: management has looked for problems and has answers prepared.

The overall tone of the document is precise, conservative, and confident without being promotional. There are no superlatives, no aspirational language about disrupting markets or achieving category leadership, and no claims that are not directly supported. This is not a weakness — it is the correct register for an audience that has been trained to distrust enthusiasm and reward rigor.

## 6. OUTCOME

The financing document was submitted jointly to ZKB and the Winterthur-based cantonal bank in September 2025. The ZKB relationship manager reported that the document "significantly simplified" the internal credit approval process — the committee had fewer questions than usual because the document had pre-addressed the standard points of inquiry. ZKB approved a CHF 5M term facility at an agreed fixed rate within six weeks of submission. The cantonal bank approved a complementary CHF 3M facility three weeks later.

Construction on the new production hall began in January 2026 on schedule. The preferred supplier agreement with the German tier-one client was formalized in December 2025, with first deliveries at expanded volume committed to begin in Q3 2026.

Markus Schuler later noted that the discipline of building the financing document had also had internal benefits: for the first time, the company's strategic case, financial narrative, and risk thinking were documented in a single place that the management team and board could reference. The document became the basis for the company's first formal strategic planning process.
