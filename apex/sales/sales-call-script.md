# APEX Sales Call Script
### 30-Minute Structure for Navid Forouzeh

---

> **Before the call:** Review the prospect's LinkedIn, website, and any prior email context. Know their stage, sector, and what they reached out about. Have a blank doc open to take notes. You are not pitching — you are diagnosing.

---

## MINUTES 0–2: OPENING

**Objective:** Establish warmth, signal peer-to-peer energy, and earn the right to ask questions.

Do not open with "So, let me tell you about APEX." Do not start with your background. Start with them.

---

### Exact Opening Lines (choose based on context)

**Version 1 — If they reached out to you:**
> "Hey [Name], good to connect. I saw your message — sounds like there's something interesting happening on your end. Tell me what's going on."

**Version 2 — If you reached out and they agreed to a call:**
> "Hey [Name], thanks for making the time. I'll keep this focused. I've looked at [company/LinkedIn/what they do] briefly — I'd rather hear it from you directly. What's the situation you're working through?"

**Version 3 — If referral or warm intro:**
> "Hey [Name], [Referrer] mentioned you — gave me just enough context to be curious. What are you working on right now?"

---

### Tone-Setting Principles

- Match their energy — if they're direct and fast, match it. If they're more reflective, slow down.
- Never say "Great!" or "Awesome!" in response to everything. It signals sales mode.
- Treat them as a peer solving a real problem, not a prospect you need to close.
- You are not nervous. You have done this before. You are genuinely curious about their situation.

---

### Brief Context Set (only if they ask, or at minute 1:30)

> "Quick context on my end — I run APEX, a strategic communication studio. We build investor decks and executive narratives. But before I say anything about that — I want to understand your situation first."

One sentence. Then ask a question. Do not elaborate unprompted.

---

## MINUTES 2–8: DISCOVERY

**Objective:** Understand their real situation — not just the surface request. Ask more than you talk. This section determines whether and how to position APEX.

You are listening for: urgency, stakes, what they've already tried, whether they have the decision-making authority, and whether this is a real project or exploratory.

---

### The 6 Discovery Questions

Ask these conversationally — not as a checklist. Follow the thread of their answers. You won't always ask all six in order.

---

**Question 1 — The Situation**
> "Walk me through what you're trying to accomplish. What's the deck — or whatever it is you're building — actually for?"

*Listen for:* What is the specific deliverable and occasion? Investor pitch? Board update? Partnership deck? Is the purpose clear in their mind or vague?

---

**Question 2 — The Stakes**
> "What happens if this goes well? And what's at risk if it doesn't land the way you need it to?"

*Listen for:* How much is on the line. A CHF 500K seed round feels different from a CHF 20M Series B. A board meeting with a skeptical chair is different from a routine update. High stakes = higher urgency = more motivated to invest in quality.

---

**Question 3 — Timeline**
> "When does this need to be ready — and is that a hard deadline or flexible?"

*Listen for:* Real urgency vs. artificial urgency. "We have a partner meeting in three weeks" is hard. "At some point in Q2" is soft. Hard deadlines = you can price for urgency. Soft = you can work at a sustainable pace and set expectations clearly.

---

**Question 4 — What They've Already Tried**
> "Have you started anything on this already? What does the current state look like?"

*Listen for:* Do they have a rough deck that needs rebuilding? A Word doc with notes? Nothing? Understanding the starting point tells you how much work is involved and how attached they might be to existing content.

---

**Question 5 — The Communication Challenge (the real one)**
> "What's the part of this that's frustrating you most, or that feels hardest to get right?"

*Listen for:* This is where founders reveal the real problem. Common answers: "I can't explain what we do simply," "the story doesn't flow," "it looks amateur," "investors seem confused about our model," "we have too much content and don't know what to cut." The answer here becomes the spine of your diagnosis.

---

**Question 6 — What Good Looks Like**
> "If you walked out of that meeting or sent that deck and it worked exactly the way you hoped — what does that look like specifically?"

*Listen for:* Their definition of success. Some people want investor responses. Some want to feel confident presenting. Some want to impress a specific person. Knowing this tells you what outcome to aim at — and how to frame your value at the end.

---

### How to Handle a Rambling Founder

Some founders use the discovery call to process their thoughts out loud. This is useful information, but you need to guide them.

When they start going in circles or providing more context than necessary:

> "That's really helpful context. Let me make sure I'm getting the core of it — the main thing you need to solve for is [X], right?"

Or:

> "I want to make sure we use this time well — can I ask you one more specific thing before we go further?"

Never interrupt rudely. Always acknowledge before redirecting. The goal is to feel like a focused, high-caliber conversation partner.

---

## MINUTES 8–16: DIAGNOSIS

**Objective:** Show that you understood not just what they said — but what it means. A great diagnosis is worth more than any portfolio piece. It demonstrates expertise before you've done a single slide.

---

### Reflecting Back What You Heard

Start with a brief summary. Make it specific — not generic.

> "Okay, let me make sure I've got this right. You're going into [meeting type] in [timeframe], with [audience type]. Right now you have [current state of materials]. The piece that's giving you the most trouble is [specific challenge they named]. And the outcome you're hoping for is [their definition of success]. Does that track?"

Wait for confirmation. Let them correct you if needed. This step alone builds more trust than a ten-minute pitch.

---

### The Diagnostic Reframe

After confirming your summary, name the deeper issue. Most founders think they have a design problem or a content problem. The real problem is almost always a narrative problem.

The diagnostic reframe takes their surface complaint and translates it into the actual communication architecture challenge — which is where your expertise lives.

---

### Example Diagnostic Statements

**Scenario 1 — "Our deck feels all over the place / lacks flow"**
> "What I'm hearing is that the structure isn't carrying the story. The content might all be accurate, but there's no through-line that pulls an investor from curiosity to conviction. That's not a design fix — that's a narrative architecture problem. Before any slide is designed, we'd need to establish what the core tension is and how you resolve it."

---

**Scenario 2 — "Investors seem confused about what we do"**
> "What I'm hearing is a positioning clarity problem. When investors are confused, it usually means the deck is answering the wrong question first — you're explaining the product before you've established why the problem matters. That's extremely common in technical founder decks, and it's fixable. We'd reorder the story to build the context before the solution."

---

**Scenario 3 — "It looks unprofessional / like a startup deck"**
> "What I'm hearing is partly a visual credibility gap — but honestly, the bigger issue is usually that design problems reflect underlying structural problems. When slides look cluttered, it's often because there's too much being said on each one, which means the narrative hasn't been distilled enough. We'd tackle the story architecture first, then the design follows naturally."

---

**Scenario 4 — "We have too much content and don't know what to cut"**
> "What I'm hearing is a prioritization problem — you're close to the company, so everything feels essential. But investors don't need everything — they need the right things in the right order. Part of what APEX does is make those editorial decisions so you don't have to. A great deck is defined as much by what's removed as what's included."

---

### Establishing That You Understand Their World

Where relevant, demonstrate that you know the investor landscape, the sector dynamics, or the specific audience they're presenting to.

> "The typical [sector] investor in [Switzerland / Europe / US] is going to want to see [specific investor concern] addressed very early. If you don't address [concern], that's where you lose the room before you've had a chance to show the upside."

Keep this brief — one or two observations. It shows fluency without turning into a lecture.

---

## MINUTES 16–22: CREDIBILITY + SOLUTION

**Objective:** Show — not tell — that APEX has the methodology and the track record to solve what you just diagnosed. Be factual. Be brief. Then describe the solution.

---

### When and How to Mention McKinsey Exposure

Do not open with this. It comes up only if the conversation has established context and credibility is relevant to close. It should feel like a natural data point — not a name drop.

> "Part of my background — I've had exposure to how strategy communication works at the McKinsey level, where every deck is built around a single compelling hypothesis and every slide earns its place. That's the standard I apply, even for early-stage companies."

That's it. One sentence. Do not elaborate unless they ask. The goal is to signal that you operate at a high-caliber intellectual standard — not to impress them with the name.

---

### The APEX Methodology (3 Steps)

Describe this clearly and simply. Do not use jargon.

> "The way I work is in three phases. First, we diagnose — I look at your business, your audience, and your current materials, and I identify the exact communication gaps. Second, we architect — we build the narrative structure before touching any slides. The story has to be right before the design begins. Third, we build — I produce the deck with both the narrative and the visual design locked in, so you receive something complete, not a template with your logo on it."

Pause after this. Let it land.

---

### The AI Velocity Point

If cost or timeline comes up — or proactively if it's relevant:

> "One thing worth knowing: APEX uses AI-augmented production — which means I can move significantly faster than a traditional agency, and at a lower cost, without compromising on quality. The strategic thinking, the narrative architecture, the editorial judgment — that's all human. The production velocity is where AI gives you an advantage. You're not waiting six weeks for a deck."

---

### What APEX Does Not Do

Naming your constraints creates trust — it signals you know what you're good at and won't overpromise.

> "What I don't do: I'm not a branding studio, I don't do logo design, and I don't manage your investor outreach process. What I do is build the communication asset that makes your outreach land. That's the scope."

---

### Showing a Showcase Example (Optional)

If they ask, or if it feels right:

> "I can share a relevant example — not to show off, but because it's often useful to see what the finished product actually looks like. I have [a seed-stage deck / a board presentation / a Series A narrative] I can walk you through briefly. Would that be useful?"

Never force the portfolio. If they're already convinced, don't create doubt by introducing new material.

---

## MINUTES 22–27: OFFER + PRICING

**Objective:** Present the right package clearly, frame the investment against the stakes they told you, and give them a number with confidence.

---

### How to Present Offers

Never open with the price. Open with the problem, then the fit, then the price.

> "Based on what you've described — [brief summary of their situation] — the right fit is [package name]. Here's what that looks like..."

---

### Exact Pricing Language

**For the SIGNAL package:**
> "For what you're describing, we'd work with the SIGNAL package — that's CHF 2,500, and it includes the narrative architecture, full visual design, one round of structured revision, and the final delivery in both PDF and editable format. That's built for founders who need a clean, investor-ready pitch without the overhead of a full agency engagement."

**For the ARCHITECT package:**
> "Given the complexity here — [specific complexity they named: multiple audiences, complex business model, board + investor version] — I'd recommend ARCHITECT. That's CHF 4,500, and it includes everything in SIGNAL plus a deeper research and diagnostic phase, a second revision round, and speaker notes built into the final deck. For a [Series A / board / partnership] context, that's where the additional depth pays off."

**For custom scope:**
> "What you're describing is a bit beyond the standard packages — there's [specific complexity]. I'd scope that custom, but we'd be working in the CHF 4,500–6,500 range depending on what we confirm on a kickoff call. I can give you an exact number after we've aligned on scope."

---

### How to Frame the Price Against the Stakes

> "You mentioned there's [amount at stake / decision on the line]. The way I think about this: a deck that doesn't land costs you that conversation, and often the next one. CHF 2,500 in that context isn't an expense — it's the cheapest lever available to increase the probability of that meeting going the way you need it to."

Or:

> "You've spent months building this company. The deck is where that work either lands or gets dismissed in the first ten minutes. The investment in getting that communication right is a fraction of what a bad impression costs you."

---

### What Not to Do

- Do not say "I know this might seem like a lot" — it signals that you think it's too much.
- Do not offer a discount unprompted. If they push back on price, handle it as an objection (see below).
- Do not over-justify. State the price once, confidently, then stop talking.

---

## MINUTES 27–30: CLOSE OR NEXT STEP

**Objective:** Get a clear answer — yes, no, or a defined next step. Do not leave with ambiguity.

---

### The Closing Question

Not a hard close. A check-in.

> "Does this feel like the right fit for what you're working on?"

Or:

> "Based on what we've talked through — does this seem like the direction you'd want to go?"

Then: stop talking. Let them respond. The first person to speak after the closing question loses momentum.

---

### If Yes

> "Great. Here's what happens next: I'll send you a brief proposal today — it'll include scope, deliverables, timeline, and the project agreement. Once that's signed and the deposit is confirmed, we schedule the kickoff call and I get started. You should expect to have the first draft within [X] business days of kickoff."

Move fast. Send the proposal within the hour. Every hour of delay reduces close probability.

---

### If Not Sure / They Need to Think

Acknowledge without pressure. But create a legitimate time constraint.

> "That makes sense — take the time you need. One thing I'll mention: my project schedule fills up about two weeks in advance. I'd want to confirm within 48 hours if you do want to move forward, because I'd be holding that slot for you. If the timing doesn't work right now, we can always reconnect when the moment is right — no pressure."

Then send a follow-up email within the hour (template below).

---

### If No

> "Totally fair. Can I ask — is it the timing, the scope, or something else? Just want to make sure I understand."

Listen. Don't argue. If it's price, use the price objection reframe. If it's timing, offer to reconnect. If it's genuinely not the right fit, thank them and end cleanly.

> "Understood. If things shift and this becomes relevant again, don't hesitate to reach back out. I appreciate the time."

End on a good note. A no now can become a referral or a yes in six months.

---

## OBJECTION HANDLING

> **Principle:** Every objection is information, not rejection. The goal is not to argue — it is to understand what's behind it and respond to the real concern.

---

### Objection 1: "You're quite young — do you have enough experience?"

**What it signals:** Uncertainty about risk. They're spending real money and need to feel safe. This is not personal — it's about minimizing their downside.

**The Reframe:** Age is a proxy for capability, and an imperfect one. Redirect to evidence of actual output quality.

**Response Options:**

Version 1 (direct):
> "That's a fair thing to consider. What I'd say is: the work either holds up at a high standard or it doesn't — and that's independent of age. I've built communication strategy informed by McKinsey-level methodology, and the decks I produce are benchmarked against the best in the space. The way I'd suggest evaluating it is to look at the output directly. Would it help to walk through a relevant example so you can assess for yourself?"

Version 2 (reframe through client perspective):
> "I understand the instinct. Here's what I'd offer: the founders who've worked with me weren't buying my biography — they were buying a deck that performed in the room. If you want, I can show you what that looks like and you can tell me whether it meets your bar."

Version 3 (if they push further):
> "I'm 19, yes. I also have exposure to how the best strategy communication firms in the world approach this — and I've built a process that reflects that. If you're looking for decades of grey hair, I'm not the right call. If you're looking for sharp thinking and a deck that's built to the standard an investor expects — that's what I do."

---

### Objection 2: "We already have a designer / internal team"

**What it signals:** They don't understand that pitch architecture is different from design. They think this is a visual problem being solved by a visual person.

**The Reframe:** APEX is not a design service. It's a narrative and communication strategy service. Design is one output.

**Response Options:**

Version 1:
> "That's useful context. Here's the distinction I'd draw: a designer can make slides look great, but the communication strategy — the story structure, the investor logic, the argument architecture — that's a different discipline. Most internal designers aren't trained in investor narrative. That's usually where the gap shows up."

Version 2:
> "In my experience, when founders have an internal team, the bottleneck isn't usually the execution — it's knowing what story to tell and in what order. If your team has bandwidth and capability on the production side, that's great. What I bring is the strategic layer: what goes in the deck, why, and how it's framed. We can talk about whether a lighter-touch engagement makes sense."

---

### Objection 3: "Can't AI just do this? ChatGPT / Gamma can make slides"

**What it signals:** They're cost-conscious and exploring alternatives. They may not understand the difference between a template and a strategic communication asset.

**The Reframe:** AI generates content. APEX generates conviction. The gap is in the strategic judgment that makes an investor move.

**Response Options:**

Version 1:
> "They can. And if your goal is to have slides, yes — AI can produce slides. What AI can't do is understand your specific fundraising context, your investor's known concerns, what your competitors are saying, and how to build an argument that moves a specific person in a specific meeting. That editorial judgment is the value. The AI I use accelerates production — but the strategy is still the human layer."

Version 2:
> "I actually use AI in my workflow — it's part of how I move fast. The difference is that I use it as a production tool under a strategic framework, not as the decision-maker. A deck that an investor looks at and says 'this team gets it' — that's not something a prompt generates. That's built."

---

### Objection 4: "This seems expensive for our stage"

**What it signals:** Budget sensitivity + possibly a mismatch in how they're valuing the deck vs. what's at stake.

**The Reframe:** The cost of not having a strong deck is almost always higher than the cost of building one.

**Response Options:**

Version 1:
> "I hear that. Let me ask: what's the round you're going into, and what does a successful outcome look like in terms of capital? [Let them answer.] So we're talking about [their number] potentially at stake. In that context, the investment in getting the communication right is a rounding error — and it directly affects the probability of that outcome. That's how I'd think about it."

Version 2:
> "Early-stage is exactly when this matters most. Before you have a track record, the deck is your primary credibility signal. That's why the investment at this stage tends to pay back more than it does later. What's driving the concern — is it cash flow right now, or is it the overall value of the engagement?"

Version 3 (if they're genuinely tight):
> "I understand. If the budget is genuinely constrained, there might be a lighter-touch scope that makes sense — not the full architecture engagement, but something more targeted. Tell me what you actually have to work with and I'll tell you honestly if there's a version of this that makes sense for your situation."

---

### Objection 5: "We need to think about it / discuss internally"

**What it signals:** Either a genuine internal process, or hesitation they're not ready to name. Identify which one.

**The Reframe:** Acknowledge the process, but surface the real concern before they disappear.

**Response Options:**

Version 1:
> "Of course — makes sense. Before we wrap: is there a specific concern behind that, or is it genuinely a process thing? I ask because if there's something I haven't answered well, I'd rather address it now than leave it hanging."

Version 2:
> "Totally understandable. Who else is involved in the decision? And what would they need to see or hear to feel comfortable moving forward? I want to make sure they have what they need."

Version 3 (post-conversation email follow-up framing):
> "I'll send over the proposal — that way you have something concrete to share internally. I'll include the scope, deliverables, and timeline so there's no ambiguity. And I'd want to confirm within 48 hours given my schedule — I'll note that in the email."

---

### Objection 6: "We don't have budget right now"

**What it signals:** Either genuine cash constraint, or a polite no. Find out which.

**The Reframe:** If it's real, understand the timeline. If it's a polite no, surface what's actually happening.

**Response Options:**

Version 1 (test if it's real):
> "Understood. When do you see that changing? Is this a timing thing — budget opens up next quarter — or is it more open-ended?"

Version 2 (if it's soft):
> "I hear that. Can I ask — if budget weren't the constraint, would this be a clear yes? I just want to make sure I understand where things actually stand, because the next step would be different depending on the answer."

Version 3 (if timing is defined):
> "That's fair. Let's do this: I'll make a note to follow up with you in [their timeframe]. And if something changes before then — an investor meeting gets moved up, a deadline shows up — reach out directly and I'll make room. These things have a way of becoming urgent quickly."

---

### Objection 7: "Can we see more examples of your work?"

**What it signals:** Not yet convinced. They want visual proof before committing. This is healthy and easy to handle.

**The Reframe:** Show work. But show it with context — don't just send files.

**Response Options:**

Version 1:
> "Absolutely. I have a few examples I can walk you through — I'd rather do it on a quick call than just send PDFs, because context matters. A deck for a deep tech seed round looks very different from a board presentation, and I want to show you something relevant to what you're working on. Can we find 20 minutes this week?"

Version 2 (if they want something immediately):
> "I'll send you over [specific example] — it's from a [similar context]. I'd ask you to look specifically at [narrative structure / slide logic / visual quality] and tell me whether that meets your bar. Happy to talk through it afterward."

---

### Objection 8: "What if we're not happy with the result?"

**What it signals:** Fear of wasting money. They want a safety net before committing.

**The Reframe:** Your process is designed to prevent this. And you stand behind your work.

**Response Options:**

Version 1 (process framing):
> "The way I build prevents this from happening at the end. We don't go straight to slides — we align on the narrative architecture first, before anything is designed. You're approving the structure and the story before any production happens. So by the time you see the deck, we've already agreed on what it needs to say."

Version 2 (revision commitment):
> "Every engagement includes a structured revision round. That's not a formality — it's where we incorporate your feedback and sharpen what isn't working. If there are material issues with the direction, we catch and correct them there. I've never delivered a final product that a client felt was off, because we build in the alignment step before we get there."

Version 3 (direct accountability):
> "If I've genuinely missed the brief — not because your preferences changed, but because I misunderstood the objective — I fix it. That's my commitment. What I'd push back on is subjective preference that shifts after delivery. That's why we define success clearly at the start."
