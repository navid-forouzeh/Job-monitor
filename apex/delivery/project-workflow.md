# APEX Project Delivery Workflow
### Complete Repeatable System for Premium Client Engagements

---

> **Principle:** Every touchpoint communicates the quality of the work before the work is seen. How you onboard, communicate, and deliver is part of what the client is paying for.

---

## PHASE 1: CLIENT ONBOARDING
### Day 0–1

---

### 1. ONBOARDING QUESTIONNAIRE

Send this within 2 hours of receiving the signed agreement and deposit. Frame the send professionally:

> *"Before we get started, I need your help answering the questions below. The more specific your answers, the sharper the output. There are no wrong answers — your instincts about your own company are valuable input. Aim to complete this within 24 hours so we can kick off on schedule."*

---

#### SECTION A: COMPANY CONTEXT

1. What does your company do? Describe it in one sentence, as if explaining to an intelligent non-expert.
2. Who is your primary customer, and what problem do you solve for them specifically?
3. What stage is the company at? (Pre-revenue, revenue-generating, profitable — include relevant numbers)
4. What have you raised to date, and from whom? (If applicable)
5. How many people are on the team, and what are the key roles?
6. What is the single most important thing you've proven or validated so far?
7. What is the single biggest open question or risk in the business right now?

---

#### SECTION B: THE DELIVERABLE

8. What type of deliverable is this? (Investor pitch deck, board presentation, executive summary, partnership deck, other)
9. Who is the specific audience for this material? (Name the firm, board member type, partner category — be specific)
10. What is the occasion or meeting this is being built for?
11. How will this be used — as a live presentation, a leave-behind document, or both?
12. What is the ideal length? (Number of slides or pages, if you have a preference)
13. What does a successful outcome from this specific presentation look like?

---

#### SECTION C: EXISTING MATERIALS

14. Do you have a previous version of this deck or document? (Yes / No — if yes, please attach)
15. What do you like about what you currently have? (Even if rough — identify what's working)
16. What are you most unhappy with in the current version?
17. Do you have a brand style guide, approved color palette, or existing design system?
18. Do you have a company logo in vector format? (SVG or EPS preferred)
19. What other materials should I review? (Website, one-pager, previous investor materials, financial model, product demos — list and attach)

---

#### SECTION D: TONE AND POSITIONING

20. How would you describe the tone you want this deck to convey? (e.g., bold and confident, data-driven and rigorous, warm and mission-driven, technical and precise)
21. If this deck were a person, what three adjectives would describe how it comes across?
22. What do you want investors / the audience to feel by the end?
23. What is the single most important thing you want them to believe after reading or seeing this?
24. Is there anything you specifically want to avoid in terms of tone, claims, or framing?

---

#### SECTION E: COMPETITIVE CONTEXT

25. Who are your top 3–5 competitors (direct or perceived)?
26. How do you differentiate from each of them? Be specific — not just "we're better" but what specifically is different.
27. Are there any decks, companies, or brands whose communication style you admire? (Can be outside your industry)
28. Are there any decks or styles you find unconvincing or want to avoid resembling?

---

#### SECTION F: TIMELINE + LOGISTICS

29. What is the hard deadline for this deliverable? (Date of meeting, submission, or send)
30. Is there any flexibility in that deadline, or is it fixed?
31. Who is the primary point of contact for this project on your side?
32. Who else needs to approve or review the final output, and at what stage?
33. Are there any blackout dates during the project when you won't be available for review?

---

#### SECTION G: DEFINING SUCCESS

34. How will you personally evaluate whether this project was a success?
35. What would you need to see in the final deliverable to feel fully confident sending it out?
36. Is there a specific investor, board member, or stakeholder you want this to particularly resonate with? Describe what matters to them.

---

#### SECTION H: BUDGET CONFIRMATION

37. You've confirmed the [Package Name] engagement at CHF [X]. Is there any additional scope you're expecting that we haven't yet discussed? (Better to surface it now than after kickoff)

---

### 2. REQUIRED MATERIALS CHECKLIST

Send this alongside the questionnaire. Require all items before kickoff.

**The client must provide before work begins:**

- [ ] Completed onboarding questionnaire (all sections)
- [ ] Company logo (SVG or EPS vector format — not PNG from a website)
- [ ] Brand guidelines or style guide (if it exists)
- [ ] Existing deck or presentation (any format — even rough drafts)
- [ ] Company website URL
- [ ] Any relevant one-pagers, executive summaries, or prior investor materials
- [ ] Financial model or key metrics summary (for investor decks: ARR, growth rate, burn, runway, unit economics)
- [ ] Market sizing data or sources, if available
- [ ] Any press coverage, case studies, or customer references relevant to the narrative
- [ ] Signed project agreement
- [ ] Deposit payment confirmation

**Note to client:** If any of these don't exist yet, that's fine — flag it and we'll work with what's available. Missing materials affect timeline, so let's know early.

---

### 3. KICKOFF PROCESS

**When:** Scheduled within 24–48 hours of receiving completed questionnaire and materials.
**Length:** 20 minutes. No longer.
**Format:** Video call (Zoom or Google Meet). Camera on — builds trust.

---

#### Kickoff Call Agenda (20 Minutes)

| Time | Agenda Item |
|------|-------------|
| 0:00–3:00 | Welcome + quick orientation: what we're covering today |
| 3:00–8:00 | Clarify questionnaire responses (any answers that were vague or need expansion) |
| 8:00–13:00 | Align on strategic priorities: what are the top 3 things this deck must accomplish? |
| 13:00–17:00 | Confirm audience, context, and success criteria |
| 17:00–19:00 | Confirm timeline + revision process + communication protocol |
| 19:00–20:00 | Confirm what Navid will send and when |

---

#### Decisions Made on the Kickoff Call

By the end of this call, you must have confirmed:

1. The single core message the deck must leave the audience with
2. The 3 non-negotiable elements the client requires in the final product
3. The exact hard deadline and any intermediate check-in points
4. Who has final approval authority (is it just the founder, or does a co-founder/board member also sign off?)
5. How feedback will be communicated (written email / doc comments — never verbal only)
6. What "revision" means in this context (one structured round, not rolling changes)

---

#### Post-Kickoff Confirmation (Send within 2 hours)

Send a brief written summary of what was agreed. This is not a long email — it's a clear confirmation.

> *"Following up on our kickoff call — here's what we aligned on:*
>
> *Deliverable: [X]*
> *Audience: [X]*
> *Core message: [X]*
> *Hard deadline: [X]*
> *Revision process: One structured revision round, feedback via [written doc / email]*
> *First draft delivery: [Date]*
>
> *If anything looks off, let me know in the next few hours. Otherwise, I'll get started.*"

## PHASE 2: RESEARCH + ARCHITECTURE
### Day 2–4

---

### 4. RESEARCH PROCESS

Before opening any design software, complete the following research. This is what separates a strategic communication firm from a slide shop.

**Document your research in a working notes file. This informs every narrative decision.**

---

#### Company Research
- Understand the business model at a deep level: how does money flow? What's the unit economics logic?
- Identify the key claims the founder makes — which are proven, which are asserted, which need caveats
- Map the team: who is on it, why does each person matter for credibility, what gaps exist
- Understand the funding history and what it signals
- Review all materials the client sent — identify contradictions, gaps, and strengths

---

#### Market Sizing Research
- What is the genuine addressable market? Find third-party sources (Statista, industry reports, comparable public company filings)
- What approach to market sizing is most credible for this sector: top-down, bottom-up, or value-theory?
- What numbers will a sophisticated investor in this space already know? Don't present stale or obviously wrong data
- What trend lines support the timing argument (why now)?

---

#### Competitor Landscape Research
- Map the 4–6 most relevant competitors (direct, indirect, and perceived)
- Understand how each positions itself: what promise do they make, who do they target?
- Identify the genuine white space: where is the company's positioning actually differentiated?
- Find any public information on competitor funding, traction, or weaknesses

---

#### Investor / Audience Research
- If the audience is a specific investor: what is that firm's thesis? What sectors and stages do they lead? What have they passed on publicly?
- If the audience is a board: what does each member care about most? What are their professional backgrounds?
- What questions does this type of investor reliably ask at this stage?
- What are the top 3 investor concerns for this specific sector and business model?
- What does a funded company in this space typically demonstrate by this stage?

---

#### Benchmarking Best-in-Class Decks
- Review 3–5 publicly available decks from comparable companies at comparable stages (Airbnb, Sequoia, Pitch deck databases)
- Note: slide structure, what's included vs. excluded, how the market slide is handled, how the ask is framed
- Identify conventions vs. conventions worth breaking
- Identify the 2–3 things that distinguish the best decks from the mediocre ones in this category

---

### 5. NARRATIVE ARCHITECTURE PROCESS

Build the story before building any slides. The architecture document is the deliverable of this phase.

---

#### The Story Spine Method

Map the narrative to this structure before writing a single slide headline:

| Story Beat | The Question It Answers | Slide Equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| **Before** | What is the world like today? What's broken or missing? | Problem / Market Context |
| **Conflict** | Why can't the world solve this on its own? What makes this hard? | Why Now / Why This Is Hard |
| **Resolution** | What does your company do, and why is it uniquely positioned? | Solution / Product / Differentiators |
| **Transformation** | What does the world look like when you win? | Vision / Market Opportunity |
| **Proof** | Why should anyone believe you? | Traction / Team / Validation |
| **Ask** | What do you need, and what will it unlock? | The Ask / Use of Funds |

Write one sentence for each story beat before moving to slides.

---

#### How to Decide Slide Order

Apply the following three tests to the proposed slide sequence:

**Test 1 — Does each slide earn its place?**
Every slide must answer a question the previous slide created. If a slide appears without the audience having a reason to care about it, it's either in the wrong place or needs to be cut.

**Test 2 — Is tension created before resolution?**
Many founders jump to the solution before the audience understands the problem. Make sure the problem slides create genuine urgency before the product is introduced.

**Test 3 — Does the deck end with conviction, not a whimper?**
The final slides (team, ask) must carry energy. A deck that peaks at the product slide and deflates at the team slide has an architecture problem.

---

#### The 3 Questions Every Investor Asks

Build the deck to answer these in sequence, whether explicitly or implicitly:

1. **"Is this a real and large problem?"** — If the investor doesn't believe the problem is real, nothing else matters.
2. **"Is this team the right team to solve it?"** — Even if the problem is real, why you?
3. **"Can this generate the kind of return I need?"** — The market and business model must justify the investment.

Verify the narrative architecture answers all three, clearly, before proceeding.

---

#### Tension and Resolution

A flat deck with no conflict is forgettable. Create productive tension in the early slides:
- State the problem starkly — do not soften it
- Show why existing solutions fail (without naming competitors offensively)
- Make the "why now" feel genuinely urgent, not manufactured

Then resolve it cleanly with the product/solution slides. The emotional arc is: concern → curiosity → conviction.

## PHASE 3: BUILD
### Day 5–9

---

### 6. SLIDE STRUCTURE PROCESS

---

#### Template Setup
- Start from the APEX master template (not a blank file, not a generic theme)
- Import brand colors from client materials or brief — establish primary, secondary, and accent
- Set font pairings: one for headlines, one for body copy
- Set slide dimensions (16:9 standard for investor decks; confirm with client)
- Create 4–5 slide layout masters: full-bleed, split layout, content-heavy, data visualization, title/section break
- Lock the template before content is placed — changes to the template mid-build create inconsistency

---

#### Build Sequence

Build in this order — do not build linearly from slide 1:

1. **Title slide** (establishes visual identity and first impression)
2. **The Ask** (build this early so everything else is calibrated to justify it)
3. **Problem slide(s)** (most narrative-critical — get these right before building the solution)
4. **Solution / Product slides** (depends on problem being solid)
5. **Market sizing slides** (requires research to be complete)
6. **Business model slide** (depends on having clear articulation of how money flows)
7. **Traction slide** (requires confirmation of what data exists)
8. **Team slide** (format is established — fill in once structure is locked)
9. **Competitor / positioning slide**
10. **Use of funds / roadmap slides**
11. **All remaining slides** (fill in per outline)
12. **Final review for visual consistency throughout**

---

#### What to Complete First — Narrative-Dependent Slides First

The most narrative-dependent slides (Problem, Solution, Market) must be locked before the data and credential slides. If the story changes, the data slides adapt. If you build data slides first, you'll be tempted to build the story around data instead of the other way around.

---

### 7. VISUAL DESIGN PROCESS

---

#### Design Principles Applied to Every Slide

**1. Hierarchy is everything.**
The eye must know instantly what to read first. One dominant element per slide. Supporting content is visually subordinate — smaller, lighter, further from center.

**2. Density is the enemy.**
Slides with 7 bullet points are not slides — they are documents that haven't been edited. Each slide should express one idea. If a slide has more than one idea, split it.

**3. Contrast creates clarity.**
Use contrast — in size, color, weight — to signal importance. A headline in the same weight as body copy is invisible.

**4. White space is active, not passive.**
Empty space on a slide is not wasted — it is the frame that gives the content room to land. Resist the urge to fill every corner.

**5. Every element must justify its presence.**
If a visual element (icon, image, divider, colored block) cannot be explained in terms of what job it is doing, remove it.

---

#### Font Usage Rules

- Maximum 2 typefaces per deck (headline + body)
- Headline font: bold, high-legibility at large sizes (e.g., Inter, Neue Haas, DM Sans, Syne)
- Body font: clean, readable at small sizes — the same family at lighter weight, or a neutral pairing
- Never use decorative or script fonts in a professional deck
- Minimum body font size: 14pt on slides (readable on screen and projected)
- Never use all-caps for full sentences — it reduces readability and signals decoration over content

---

#### Color Usage Rules

- Primary color: company brand primary (or APEX default if none exists)
- Accent color: used sparingly for emphasis, data highlights, and key callouts
- Background: white or very light neutral (never a dark background unless brand explicitly requires)
- Text: near-black (#1A1A1A or equivalent) — never pure black, which reads as harsh
- Maximum 3 colors used actively per slide (background + primary + accent)
- Data visualizations: use a controlled palette — do not use default chart colors

---

#### Rookie Design Mistakes That Kill Credibility

Avoid these. Every one of them signals an amateur product to a sophisticated audience.

- Using default PowerPoint / Google Slides themes or default chart colors
- Misaligned elements (text boxes not aligned to a grid)
- Multiple font sizes used inconsistently across slides
- Logos placed off-center or at inconsistent sizes
- Text that runs to the edge of the slide (no margin)
- Drop shadows on text
- Clip art or stock illustration that looks generic
- Animations on every slide transition (none, or minimal and tasteful only)
- Inconsistent capitalization in headers (pick Title Case or Sentence case — not both)
- Dense bullet lists that haven't been distilled into a visual or a key claim

## PHASE 4: QUALITY CONTROL + DELIVERY
### Day 10–14

---

### 8. QUALITY CONTROL CHECKLIST

Run this checklist on the completed deck before any human sees it. No exceptions.

---

#### Narrative Flow

- [ ] Does the deck have a clear opening hook that creates immediate engagement?
- [ ] Does each slide create a question that the next slide answers?
- [ ] Is tension established before resolution — problem before solution?
- [ ] Does the deck answer: Is the problem real? Is this team right? Can this generate returns?
- [ ] Does the deck end with confidence and clarity — not trailing off?
- [ ] Is there a slide or section that can be removed without damaging the story? (If yes, cut it)
- [ ] Is the core message of the deck expressible in one sentence?

---

#### Data Accuracy

- [ ] Is every market size figure sourced? (Source cited on slide or available in appendix)
- [ ] Are all company metrics up to date and accurate to what the client confirmed?
- [ ] Is financial data consistent across all slides? (Numbers don't contradict each other)
- [ ] Are competitor claims factual and defensible?
- [ ] Are any claims made that the client hasn't confirmed or can't support in a follow-up?

---

#### Design Consistency

- [ ] Same font family used throughout — no rogue font appearing on one slide
- [ ] Same heading size used for equivalent slide elements throughout
- [ ] Color palette consistent — no random colors appearing
- [ ] Logo appears on correct slides, at correct size, properly positioned
- [ ] All text boxes aligned to the grid — no floating elements
- [ ] Slide numbers present (if required) and formatted consistently
- [ ] Margins consistent across all slides
- [ ] No slide has a visual element that is materially different in style from the rest of the deck

---

#### Investor Perspective Check

Stand in the investor's position. Read the deck once without stopping. Then answer:

- [ ] Is there anything confusing about the business model?
- [ ] Is there any claim that immediately raises a skeptical question that the deck doesn't answer?
- [ ] Is the market sizing credible and presented in a way that makes sense?
- [ ] Is it clear what the money is being used for and what that unlocks?
- [ ] Are there any slides that feel like filler — present but not earning their place?
- [ ] Is the team slide compelling, or does it feel like a bio page?
- [ ] Does the traction slide tell a story of momentum, or just list metrics?

---

#### Proofreading

- [ ] Every slide read word by word — zero typos
- [ ] No grammatical errors in any headline or body copy
- [ ] Consistent punctuation style (periods at end of bullets — yes or no, but consistent)
- [ ] Consistent capitalization style across headlines
- [ ] No placeholder text remaining (no "[Company Name]" left unfilled)
- [ ] All URLs are live and correct (if included)
- [ ] Company name spelled correctly and consistently throughout

---

#### File Formats

- [ ] Editable source file saved and backed up (PowerPoint .pptx or Keynote .key)
- [ ] PDF export generated (check that fonts render correctly in the PDF)
- [ ] File named correctly: `[CompanyName]_[DeckType]_[Date]_v1.pdf`
- [ ] File size is appropriate for email (compress if over 10MB)
- [ ] Source file is clean — no hidden slides, no draft comments remaining in the file
- [ ] Delivery format confirmed with client before sending

---

### 9. REVISION PROCESS

---

#### How to Present the First Draft

Do not just send a file. Frame the delivery.

> *"I've completed the first draft. Before you open it, a few things to have in mind:*
>
> *The deck is built around [core narrative logic — e.g., the tension between the scale of the problem and the inadequacy of current solutions]. That's the through-line you'll want to evaluate first.*
>
> *I'd suggest reading it once straight through before taking notes — get the overall feel before reacting to individual slides.*
>
> *I'll follow up with a brief call if useful, or happy to receive written feedback directly. Either works.*"

Then attach the PDF.

---

#### How to Receive Feedback Professionally

When the client sends feedback:
- Acknowledge quickly (within a few hours): *"Got it — I'll review and confirm what falls within the revision scope."*
- Never respond defensively to criticism — treat feedback as information, not judgment
- Categorize feedback internally: Is this a correction (factual error), a preference (aesthetic choice), or a scope change (new direction)?
- For legitimate revision items: implement without drama
- For scope questions: address per the language below

---

#### What Counts as a Revision vs. a Scope Change

**Revision (included):**
- Changing copy on existing slides
- Adjusting visual elements (color, layout, font sizing)
- Reordering slides
- Adding or removing minor content within the agreed structure
- Incorporating feedback on tone, clarity, or emphasis

**Scope change (additional fee):**
- Adding 3 or more new slides not in the original outline
- Changing the fundamental narrative direction or target audience
- Requesting a completely different visual style or rebrand
- Adding a new deliverable (e.g., an executive summary in addition to the deck)
- Making changes after the revision round has been completed and signed off

---

#### Exact Language for Scope Creep

> *"Happy to incorporate this. One thing I want to flag: [specific request] goes beyond what we originally scoped together — it's essentially [new slides / new direction / different format]. I want to make sure we're aligned before I build it, because that would be additional work outside the current agreement. The scope addition would be CHF [X]. Let me know if you want to proceed."*

Say it once. Don't apologize. Be direct.

---

### 10. FINAL HANDOVER PACKAGE

Deliver the following. Every item. No exceptions.

**Standard Delivery Package:**

| Item | Format | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Final deck — editable | .pptx or .key | Fully editable, all fonts embedded |
| Final deck — PDF export | .pdf | Compressed to <10MB for email |
| Usage notes | 1-page document or email | How to present, common investor questions to prepare for |
| Speaker notes (if included in scope) | Included in source file | Brief, punchy — not a script |

---

#### Usage Notes Content

The usage notes document should include:

1. **How to open the presentation:** Any technical notes (fonts required, slide size settings)
2. **Presentation guidance:** Which slides to dwell on vs. move through quickly; where to invite questions vs. push forward
3. **Likely questions to prepare for:** Based on the content of the deck, what 5 questions is the investor likely to ask? (Navid provides this based on his investor research during production)
4. **What not to say:** Any statements in the deck that need verbal qualification or context the slides don't provide
5. **File maintenance:** How to update metrics/traction slide as numbers change

---

### 11. TESTIMONIAL REQUEST

**When to ask:** 3–5 days after final delivery, once the client has had time to use the deck or share it. Do not ask at delivery — the client hasn't yet seen the work land.

**Trigger to ask sooner:** If the client sends you an unprompted positive message after the call or after sharing the deck.

---

**Exact Message to Send:**

> *Subject: One quick ask*
>
> *Hi [Name],*
>
> *Glad to hear [reference to outcome if known — e.g., the pitch went well / the deck landed well with the board].*
>
> *I have one quick ask: would you be open to sharing a short sentence or two about your experience working with APEX? Just your honest impression — what the project was like, and what the output delivered for you. It doesn't need to be long.*
>
> *You can reply directly to this email and I'll take care of formatting. It goes on the APEX website and in my client portfolio.*
>
> *No obligation — genuinely just asking.*
>
> *[Navid]*

---

**If they agree but don't follow through:** Send one gentle reminder 5 days later. No more than that.

---

### 12. UPSELL PROCESS

**When to raise the next engagement:**
- During final delivery — mention it briefly but don't push
- 2–4 weeks after delivery — follow up with a direct, low-pressure note
- When a natural trigger occurs: *"Congrats on the round close — when you're ready to build the Series B narrative, I'd love to help with that."*

---

**How to Frame the Next Engagement:**

Do not position it as a sales pitch. Position it as continuity.

> *"Now that the deck is done and you've got it in front of investors — a natural next step, when the timing is right, is [executive summary for follow-on conversations / board deck for the first post-raise board meeting / partnership narrative for the enterprise sales process]. Happy to scope that when you're ready."*

One sentence. Plant the seed. Let them come to you.

---

**Upsell Triggers by Project Type:**

| First Project | Natural Next Engagement |
|---|---|
| Seed investor deck | Executive summary + one-pager |
| Series A pitch deck | Board presentation for post-close board |
| Board presentation | Investor update template |
| One-pager | Full pitch deck |
| Investor deck | Partnership / sales deck |
